# Interfaces
This is work people will look at. When you build or change it:
- Start from what the project already has: its components, tokens, type scale, spacing and colours. Extend them; never start a second style beside them.
- Decide first what people came for: the one thing they work on or read here (a canvas, a board, a table, a form, a game, an article) and the first thing they do. That takes the first screen. A tool opens on the tool, with no hero or row of feature cards before it; a reading page gives the whole text; a landing page shows the real offer, its price and its limits. A tool or a document shows ambition through precision and restraint; a page that has to win someone over gets a full point of view.
- A page that has to win someone over starts from one idea, written down before any code: on this page, [something that belongs to the subject: what it produces, its shape, its name, its ritual, a printed thing from its world] becomes [a way of laying out the page]. Put the nearest competitor's name in the sentence: if it still works, the idea belongs to the category, so find another. Such a page has one live thing made from that idea, using the subject's real content, working by thumb on a phone and never in the way of reading.
- Every piece of text has a job and a place: labels on controls, units beside their numbers, state beside what it describes, errors at their field, a hint inside an empty area and gone once it fills, help a click away. How it was built and what you did belong in your reply, never on the page. Show what people need to decide or to avoid a mistake; do not hide it to look clean.
- The interface is made of the materials of the product's own world, the way a game's is of its story (Assassin's Creed's white lines and memory fragments are its Animus): a tea shop's are its tins, leaves, origins and steeping times, a board's are its cards and wall, a habit app's its days and ticks. Take that method, never another world's look.
- New work gets a look of its own, and colour sets its mood first. Decide what it should feel like (calm or loud, light or dark, warm or cool), picture two or three palettes with that feeling, each from somewhere specific (a place at a time of day, an object, a print, a film, a painting), and choose the best. Look past the first idea: generated pages keep landing on cream with sage, forest green or terracotta, on white and slate with a blue button, and on navy with neon cyan and violet. One ground, one ink, one accent, and the accent goes on the main action and at most one other thing. The ground holds the whole page and turns once or twice at most, where the page itself turns (one inverted section, the close), never section by section: stripes of alternating fields are the template showing. Words on a coloured field are set in the ink or the paper, never in a tint of the field, and no colour joins that the palette does not name. Each colour is clearly a colour or clearly a neutral: greyed mid-tones (olive-grey, brown-grey) look dirty. Running text in the full ink, since a page set in soft grey reads unfinished. Surfaces are told apart by a step in tone. In a game, what is in play is the brightest thing on screen. Build each colour as lightness steps in OKLCH.
- Two or three sizes do the visible work: one large, three to five times the body (six at most), one small, and five at most on the page. Body text on a page that persuades is 17 to 19px (Chinese 16 to 18px), with line height 1.3 to 1.5 for Latin text and 1.6 to 1.8 for Chinese. These are the figures of well-made brand sites, measured: they use five sizes where generated pages use twelve. Headlines take two lines or fewer at every width. One type family, or a pairing with a reason. Text is left-aligned and centred at most once. Text blocks share two or three left edges. Equal outer margins, and no section huddled to one side of an empty half. The gap between sections is clearly larger than any gap inside one, and each section is composed differently from its neighbour.{{paint}}
- Avoid what the best sites rarely have and generated ones nearly always do: icon-topped feature cards and rows of equal cards, pill badges, a small label above a heading, gradients, shadows on things at rest, glows, tiny letter-spaced capitals, 01/02/03 numbering, monospace labels, hairline rules, glass panels, emoji as icons, cards inside cards, a coloured word in a headline.
- Words sit on the ground. Well-made sites box a tenth of their text, generated pages half of it: plans, prices, quotes, steps and questions are type in rows and columns parted by space, and a box is kept for what is an object in its own right (a form to fill, the product's own screen, the one plan singled out). A box dressed as a note, a ticket, a tag or a sheet of paper is still a box, and nothing that is read is tilted.
- What can be pressed and what is only looked at never share a look. Each kind of control is drawn one way throughout, with hover, focus and pressed states, and nothing else borrows it: no tick boxes, dials, switches, tabs, arrows or underlines that do nothing, and no words that explain how to operate the page. On every screen it is plain what the next step is and where to take it: one main action, in the same words and the same look wherever it returns.
- Every part earns its place with the visitor: it helps them understand, decide or act, or it goes. Name what they do with a section, a strip, a counter, a row of three points or an ornament before adding it. Nothing is invented to fill: no made-up figures or live counters, serial numbers, stamps, founding years or running strips of slogans.
- The first screen every model makes is a headline with a short paragraph and a button on the left and a picture on the right. Do not make it: the direction or the idea decides what the first screen is, and the words sit inside that.
- Taking those away is half the job. A page that loses them and gains nothing reads as thin, and what fills it is pictures and real content at a larger size, never more parts: well-made brand sites give real pictures half or more of the page, generated ones almost none. Take a page's pictures in this order:
  - photos the user gave or the project has (link a picture only from a file in the project or an address you have seen load, since stock-photo addresses remembered from training are mostly dead);
  - for software, its own interface built in HTML with believable data, whole or tilted a little with its own shadow, as the main picture;
  - a picture computed from the subject's real data: a map of its origins, a calendar of its streaks, a chart of its figures, a rendering in characters;
  - type set large, with the full content of every section.
  Do not draw illustrations, scenes or characters for a page's own look: one a model draws badly costs more taste than it adds. Never a placeholder shape.
- A screen people work in (a dashboard, an inbox, an editor, settings, a list, a player) is judged by how fast the eye finds what matters and how long it can stay without tiring:
  - Name the screen's one question ("which deploy broke?", "who is waiting for a reply?") and the one finding in today's data. The largest block answers the question; the finding sits under the title, beside the action it calls for. Every other block answers a next question, or goes.
  - The title is a noun at about 24px (the product, the object, "Overview"): no greeting, no hero, no welcome banner, no paragraph about the screen. The work starts on the first screen: the list, the board, the figures, the player.
  - A state is shown beside the thing it belongs to, in words with a small mark, and only when someone would act on it. No blinking or pulsing dots, no "Live" or "System online" badge in a corner; freshness is a plain time ("Updated 10:42") where it matters.
  - Calm to look at for hours: body text 14 to 16px with line height near 1.5, ink near #1a1a1a on a ground near #f7f7f7 (or #e6e6e6 on #121212 when dark), never pure black on white or white on black, no saturated ground or sidebar. One accent: on the main data and at most one control. Panels told apart by a step in tone or one quiet border.
  - Figures in tabular numerals, right-aligned in columns, 24 to 30px at most for the key ones, in the ink colour. Peer figures share one divided container, with no icon tiles and no sparklines. A chart draws one hue as a 2px line or flat bars, with three to five faint gridlines and no legend for a single series. Table headers in sentence case, 12px grey, no zebra stripes.
  - Small changes move: selection, opening, saving and new rows ease in over 150 to 250 ms. Nothing at rest moves.
- Design every state: empty, loading, error, long text, many items, a narrow screen.
- Text fits and wraps, nothing overflows at 390 or 1280 px wide, controls are at least 40 px to tap, contrast meets WCAG AA.
- Motion has a job (feedback, where something came from or went): animate transform and opacity only, 150 to 250 ms with ease-out for feedback and up to 400 ms for entrances, one easing family, and a calm version under prefers-reduced-motion. A page that persuades may have one signature moment of motion made from the subject's own material (the product turning as you scroll, its map filling in, its interface working by itself), and nothing else that moves on its own: never the same fade-in on every section, numbers that count up, or bounce.
- A game runs on requestAnimationFrame with delta time, fits its field of play, score and controls on one screen as it opens (1280×800 and 390×844), takes the keyboard and touch or pointer input, has start, pause, game-over and restart states, answers every action with feedback (easing, a small shake, particles), scales its canvas by devicePixelRatio and keeps game keys from scrolling the page. A swipe or drag on touch needs touch-action (pan-y or none) on its area, or the browser takes the gesture for scrolling and cancels the pointer.
