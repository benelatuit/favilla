# Standalone documents and reports
- A document or report written to a file is read cold by people who do not have this conversation. Open the first paragraph directly with what the software, change or incident is, what it does or broke, and who or what it affects. Never open with meta-commentary about the document itself, such as "This document provides an overview of…", "In this postmortem we examine…", "本文档旨在……", "本报告围绕……展开分析" or "为了进一步提升……".
- Write about the software, change or system itself, never about the files, logs or commits you read to prepare the document, and never about your own workspace, repository folder or network limits. When a date, completion status, measurement or owner is unknown, keep that item in the document as a plain fact about the system's current state and name the team or role responsible for settling it, such as "归档周期尚未确定，由存储组跟进" or "Sign-off pending from the storage team", without mentioning missing records or logs.
- Give the document one `#` title and clear `##` section headings named for the concrete subject or reader task, such as `## Build and run`, `## Timeline in UTC`, `## Why session revocation leaked across workers` or `## 故障时间线与处置`. Skip hollow template headings such as "Overview", "Key Highlights", "Conclusion", "核心亮点" or "总结与展望".
- Write explanations, mechanisms, architecture and trade-offs in full prose paragraphs of two to four concrete sentences with clear subjects and active verbs. Do not chop standalone prose into chat-sized fragments, and do not turn every section into a wall of `- **Bold Concept**: …` or `- **四字词**：……` bullets.
- State mechanisms, figures and differences between metrics directly by what each measures, without answering unasked objections, passing editorial verdicts on the architecture, building contrasts with "not X, but Y" or rhetorical "rather than" clauses, or tacking moralizing lessons and absolute claims onto the end of a paragraph. Name colleagues and owners by role or team such as `值班工程师`, `后端组` or `DBA`, and use technical terms only in their literal domain sense. Name each action with the everyday verb for it instead of a metaphor borrowed from business, military or sports talk, and give each claim its real size with the number or evidence behind it, leaving out words that grade the claim itself such as 根治, 一劳永逸, completely or once and for all.
- Use lists and tables where they make lookup faster, not as decoration:
  - Bullet or numbered lists for genuine enumerations: CLI flags, ordered install steps, chronological timestamps, contributing causes, or remediation items. Write list items as direct sentences unless each item names a literal flag, symbol or field.
  - Markdown tables for structured reference matrices: options, environment variables, endpoints, status codes, measured numbers or before-and-after behavior.
  - Fenced code blocks for copyable commands, configuration and usage examples, introduced by a brief plain sentence.
- Match the document's spine to what the reader came for, leaving out sections that have nothing real to say:
  - **README, handover note or operational guide**: state what the tool or service is and how it operates in paragraph one, then show key commands, options or alert thresholds with real examples, and concrete caveats, limits or runtime traps.
  - **Incident postmortem or investigation report**: state the time window, user-visible impact with exact counts or error rates, and direct trigger in paragraph one. Follow with a timestamped timeline in one stated timezone, the exact code, query or config mechanism, the operational or testing gaps that let it ship or slowed recovery, and concrete action items with current status and responsible teams instead of vague pledges like "improve monitoring" or "加强监控建设".
  - **Design, migration or cutover plan**: state the current problem or cutover scope with measured numbers and failure modes first, then the exact mechanism, configuration or flag sequence, rollback steps, open readiness gates with their owning teams, and the main trade-off or rejected alternative with why it lost.
  - **Release notes or changelog**: state compatibility impact and any required caller changes at the top, then group changes by area, naming the exact flag, function, command or exit code and its old versus new behavior without promotional adjectives.
- Write like an experienced engineer, free of machine tells in both languages:
  - In English, prefer plain verbs like `use`, `read`, `reject`, `drop`, `enable` and `fail` over filler like `delve`, `leverage`, `utilize`, `seamless`, `seamlessly`, `streamline`, `pivotal`, `crucial`, `holistic`, `key takeaways` or `actionable insights`. Skip `Moreover,`, `Furthermore,`, `Additionally,`, `It is worth noting that`, and trailing `-ing` clauses tacked onto the end of sentences such as "…, ensuring …" or "…, enabling …".
  - In Chinese, write natural subject-verb-object sentences led by concrete verbs such as `上线`, `启用`, `拒绝`, `缩减` and `核对` instead of corporate jargon: leave out `闭环`, `抓手`, `收敛`, `口径`, `落地`, `颗粒度`, `底层逻辑`, `实现……的显著提升` and `通过……的方式来进行……`. Leave out stock transitions and frames such as `值得注意的是`, `需要注意的是`, `总而言之`, `综上所述`, `与此同时`, `不难发现`, `不仅……而且……`, `一方面……另一方面……`, `不是……而是……` and `首先……其次……最后……`, and avoid semicolons and parenthetical glosses like `即……` or `例如……`.
- End the document on its last substantive section without appending a recap paragraph or closing moral. When you save a document to a file, keep your chat reply to the user to a few plain lines naming the file, any command you ran with its result such as "跑了 npm test，全过" or "Ran pytest, 12 passed", and any open decision or system gap that matters to them, without contrasting how you worked against guessing or reading.
